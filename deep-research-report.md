A useful theoretical starting point is Scaife and Rogers’ argument that diagrams, maps, animations, and related graphics are best understood as **external cognitive artifacts** rather than prettier substitutes for text. Their critique was that graphical representations change the work a user has to do; they can offload memory, restructure search, and make relationships available “at a glance,” but only if they are carefully designed. ## Stable geography beats flexible geometry

The spatial-memory literature is surprisingly consistent on one point: what helps is usually **stable spatial arrangement**, not “more dimensionality” in the abstract. In Data Mountain, Robertson and colleagues explicitly designed a document space to exploit spatial memory with a **fixed viewpoint**, passive landmarks, and user-controlled placement. Their study found statistically reliable advantages over Internet Explorer Favorites, and the system’s fixed view was a deliberate design choice so users did not have to navigate the space itself before they could remember where things were. citeturn13view0turn14view0turn15view0

That does **not** mean “3D is better.” Cockburn and McKenzie’s follow-up work is one of the most important corrective papers in this area. They compared 2D and 3D physical and virtual arrangements and found that performance **deteriorated** as users gained more freedom to place items in a third dimension; participants also judged those higher-dimensional interfaces to be more cluttered and less efficient. In other words, the benefit comes from spatial memory and stable indexing, not from adding depth, camera movement, or freedom for its own sake. citeturn13view1turn14view1turn15view2

This is exactly where the old “visual momentum” literature becomes relevant. Bennett and Flach’s redux of Woods’ 1984 concept defines visual momentum as the degree to which an interface supports people as they move between information-seeking activities across screens, within screens, and within individual displays. Read against your project, that strongly supports your idea of persistent north/west/center/east/south territories: a user should not have to cognitively reboot every time they move from command intent to execution flow to receipts to history. A stable geography is a performance tool, not a style choice. citeturn8search14turn8search18

## Landmarks are functional memory aids

The landmark literature is even more explicit. Vinson’s CHI paper on navigating virtual environments recommends several landmarks, not just one, and specifically argues for including all five of Lynch’s classic types in some form: paths, edges, districts, nodes, and landmarks. Just as important, Vinson argues that landmarks should remain visible at all navigable scales, should be distinctive from nearby objects, and should be asymmetrical enough that users can infer orientation rather than merely recognize that they have seen something before. citeturn13view4turn36view0turn36view1turn36view3turn36view4

That speaks directly to your “spatial geography” doctrine. A north command zone, west execution strip, center causality map, east governance zone, and south replay shelf are not arbitrary metaphors. They behave like districts and paths in a cognitive map. A reasonable synthesis of Vinson plus visual-momentum work is that if these regions are stable, visually distinct, and legible across zoom levels and cognition layers, users will start building muscle memory for where kinds of meaning live. That is a research-grounded claim, not just a designer’s intuition. citeturn12search2turn36view1turn36view4turn8search14

The more recent GUI-landmark work makes this even less speculative. In their 2021 CHI paper, Uddin and Gutwin found that frequent users relied heavily on landmarks already present in interfaces, especially **layout, command groups, corners, edges, and icon visuals**, to remember where commands were. They identified four landmark types and reported that when command-location tasks used sketches or “washed-out” interfaces that preserved structure while removing content, overall pointing accuracy rose to roughly 73–74%, precisely because outlines, borders, and shapes still provided landmarks. That is a powerful result for Rig: people can navigate and remember using structure alone, provided the structure is stable and distinctive. citeturn15view3turn37view0turn37view4

The 2017 artificial-landmarks paper pushes in the same direction. Uddin, Gutwin, and Cockburn tested spatially stable grid menus and found that for medium and large grids, simple artificial anchor marks significantly improved performance; in fact, those simple anchors were faster and less error-prone than a richer transparent background image. The implication is subtle but important: the best landmark is often not the fanciest one. It is the one that is **simple, stable, easy to parse, and easy to encode into memory**. citeturn38view0

## Explanations work when layered and governed

On annotations and educational overlays, the literature is equally clear that text is not an afterthought. Rahman and colleagues’ recent survey of annotations in information visualization synthesizes empirical work showing that annotations can improve engagement, interaction, comprehension, and memorability. Hearst’s more recent review goes even further and argues that language should be treated as co-equal with visualization, not as mere captioning attached after the “real” design is done. citeturn10view12turn22view0turn23view0

But the papers also warn that text is powerful enough to be dangerous. Hearst summarizes results showing that when captions point to less visually salient but still plausible parts of a chart, people often remember the captioned region rather than the visually dominant one; in her phrasing, **the text overruled the visuals**. Stokes, Xiong Bearfield, and Hearst then showed that annotations had only a small effect on how viewers predicted data trends, but a **significant** effect on how biased they perceived the author to be. So the research answer is not “add more annotations.” It is “govern explanatory text carefully, because it can redirect interpretation and social judgment.” citeturn46view5turn22view0turn43search1turn43search3

The stronger empirical results on “how much text” are also interesting. In the chart-and-text studies summarized by Hearst and detailed by Stokes and colleagues, heavily annotated charts were **not** punished by readers simply for having more text. In fact, participants often preferred the more fully annotated versions, as long as the text was relevant and non-redundant; in one comparison, 14% of participants even preferred the all-text condition, and a significant minority preferred text-only over charts in some tasks. That means your instinct to build a “systems professor” layer is supported by evidence, but only if the text adds semantic value rather than repeating what the eye already knows. citeturn46view4turn44search3turn22view0

The educational-psychology literature explains why. Mayer and Moreno’s multimedia-learning work argues that cognitive load is central, not peripheral, to interface design. Their review reports a strong **segmentation effect** for learner-controlled chunks of animation and a **signaling effect** when cues such as headings, pointers, arrows, and stressed keywords guide attention to the relevant parts of a causal process. The underlying lesson for Rig is that educational narration should appear as small, well-timed, controllable bursts that highlight causal structure, not as dense blocks of prose laid over a live system. citeturn13view5turn15view6turn16view2turn16view3

The paper that comes closest to your exact idea is Martinez-Maldonado and colleagues’ layered storytelling work. They argue that simply rendering multiple streams is naïve; instead, multimodal data should be organized into **meaningful layers** that explain critical insights. Their principles are remarkably aligned with your doctrine: each layer should show one story at a time, layers should share a static background frame with common landmarks, users should be able to select and combine layers, and each layer should be defined by the underlying assessment or pedagogical intention. This is not a generic dashboard recipe. It is essentially a research-backed case for progressive cognitive disclosure. citeturn27view0turn28view0turn46view0turn46view2

The narrative-visualization literature adds one important caveat. Segel and Heer’s canonical paper described narrative visualization as often moving from an **author-driven** phase into a **reader-driven exploratory** phase. Boy, Detienne, and Fekete later found that adding introductory stories can increase readers’ interest, but has limited impact on memorability and on motivating deeper exploration. More recently, Shao and colleagues reported that data storytelling improved the **efficiency** of comprehension tasks and improved effectiveness when tasks centered on a **single insight**, but the benefits were not universal across all task types. Put together, the research says that explainers are best treated as ramps into reasoning, not replacements for reasoning. citeturn30search9turn4search2turn31view0

## Motion should preserve continuity, not decorate

The animation literature is one of the clearest places where your “meaningful motion” doctrine matches the papers. Heer and Robertson found that well-designed animated transitions can significantly improve graphical perception. Their rationale is exactly the one you have been circling around: motion attracts attention, supports **object constancy**, and helps viewers understand how one state became another. But they also warn that abusing motion can create false relations, false causality, and temporal chart junk. citeturn33view0turn34view0turn46view3

That matches the more skeptical learning literature. Tversky, Morrison, and Bétrancourt’s review found that animation is **not automatically superior** to static graphics and is most likely to help when it supports real-time reorientation in time and space rather than when it merely shows a complex process moving around. In practical terms, that means a pulse, highlight, or preserving transition that answers “what changed?” or “where did this go?” is research-aligned, whereas ornamental ambient motion is mostly noise. citeturn41search0turn41search1

The recent animated-data-graphics work sharpens that design guidance. Thompson, Liu, and Stasko argue that effective animated explanation requires control over **staging** and **staggering**: complex transitions should be broken into smaller sub-transitions, and objects should not all move at once if simultaneous motion creates confusion. Their system design is aimed at authoring expressive visual narratives, but the principle generalizes to runtime systems: if you want people to see execution propagation, governance blocking, or replay restoration, the motion should be staged around causal units and identity-preserving matches, not emitted as a global shimmer. citeturn33view1turn34view1

## Implications for Rig

The papers point to a fairly crisp synthesis for Rig. Your spatial navigation control should not be a free-flying camera or an ornamental minimap. It should preserve a **static reference frame**, expose **persistent districts** of meaning, use **simple asymmetrical landmarks** that survive disclosure levels and zoom, and avoid re-layouts that break users’ accrued memory. That synthesis follows directly from Data Mountain, Cockburn’s 2D-vs-3D results, Vinson’s landmark rules, Uddin’s GUI-landmark studies, and the visual-momentum literature. citeturn13view0turn15view2turn36view3turn37view4turn8search14

Your educational-annotation layer should be designed as a governed signaling system. The literature supports short, local, relevance-tested explainers that point to a specific salient event, preserve the surrounding spatial frame, and can be stepped, dismissed, or revisited by the user. It does **not** support constantly-on text that editorializes the whole display at once. If you want the explainers to teach without distorting, each overlay should be narrowly scoped, visibly anchored, phrased neutrally, and segmented into one causal claim at a time. That is the intersection of the annotation, bias, multimedia-learning, and layered-storytelling papers. citeturn10view12turn46view5turn43search1turn15view6turn46view2

Your semantic-motion doctrine is also on strong ground, but only under constraint. Motion should indicate preservation of identity, transition of state, or propagation of causality. Success pulses, governance shakes, and replay re-projections make sense **if** they are tied to genuine runtime events and maintain object constancy. The literature is against decorative motion for its own sake and against any transition that makes unrelated states feel causally continuous. In other words, your motion system is strongest when it behaves like evidence. citeturn33view0turn46view3turn41search1turn33view1

The deepest takeaway is that your “spatial geography” and “educational narration” ideas are not side quests. They are what the HCI and visualization literature would predict for a tool whose job is to let users **see governed software thinking over time**. Spatial structure reduces search and supports cognitive maps. Landmarks turn layout into memory. Layered explanation reduces overload. Text can steer meaning, so it must be governed. Motion can reveal causality, so it must preserve truth. The research does not merely allow your direction. It strongly suggests that this is the right direction, provided you keep the substrate stable and make every explanatory or animated element answerable to cognition rather than spectacle. citeturn9search1turn36view4turn27view0turn16view3turn31view0turn33view0
